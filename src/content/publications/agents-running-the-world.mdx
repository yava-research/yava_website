The useful test for an AI agent is simple: can it finish a task for me, safely, with less effort than doing it myself? Searching and summarizing are only the beginning. To complete real-world work, an agent has to navigate third-party services, respect commercial constraints, obtain verifiable permission, carry a payment credential, and recover when something goes wrong. That makes distribution, payment rails, and permissioned access to services just as important as the underlying model.

<Callout title="The thesis" tone="thesis">
  Frontier models are necessary, not sufficient. The real bottlenecks to an agentic economy are distribution inside existing user-merchant ecosystems, tokenized payment rails that preserve dispute rights, and negotiated access to third-party APIs. Today, consumer transaction volume through general-purpose agents remains tiny, while incumbents are actively throttling unapproved bots to protect their customer relationships and unit economics.
</Callout>

## The landscape: models are only the front end

A wave of consumer and developer agent surfaces has arrived from the major labs and platforms — including OpenAI's Bots, Meta's Muse, xAI and SpaceX's Grok Bot, and Instinct — alongside growing interest in what [a16z has framed as the "B2A" (business-to-agent) economy](https://www.a16z.news/p/welcome-to-the-b2a-economy).

The limitation is rarely the reasoning step. An agent can compare products or draft an itinerary in seconds, yet stall at the moment of execution because it lacks three things: a trusted distribution channel where merchants already participate, an authorized way to pay, and permission to read or write to external systems.

## Distribution: why existing ecosystems start ahead

![Tencent's AI agent interface inside Weixin showing Mini Program product discovery and ordering](/publications/tencent_ai_agents.jpeg)

[Tencent's October 2026 results](https://www.tencent.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/c700_Joshua-PS-Publication-Announcement.pdf) describe Xiaowei as a small-scale agent prototype inside Weixin that uses Mini Programs to help users discover products, compare options, and place orders. An existing super-app ecosystem gives Tencent a clear distribution advantage: users, merchants, and payment credentials already meet inside the same application.

Even so, Xiaowei is an early prototype, not proof that autonomous commerce has reached scale. A large installed base lowers the cost of experimentation, but habit formation depends on trust and merchant participation. The metrics worth watching are repeat usage, completed transactions, error and refund rates, and how often a human has to step in to rescue a broken flow.

## Reality check: early consumer adoption is thin

Despite the hype around [agentic commerce](https://mdm.co/agentic-commerce-is-a-mirage-part-5/) — a skepticism echoed in [industry commentary on X](https://x.com/eric_seufert/status/2124888248153423889) — disclosed transaction volumes from major consumer platforms remain minuscule:

- **Food delivery:** In late October 2026, [DoorDash disclosed](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/30/doordash-said-its-only-received-several-hundred-orders-from-ai-agents/) that it had received only several hundred orders from AI agents in total. On paper, food delivery is an ideal test case: preferences recur, inventories update in real time, and a cart can be reviewed before checkout. In practice, an agent still has to handle out-of-stock substitutions, allergies, delivery addresses, and layered fees. The right benchmark is not prompt volume, but the share of orders completed accurately without expensive customer support.
- **Ridesharing:** In early November 2026, [Uber reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/04/almost-no-one-uses-ai-agents-to-book-their-uber-company-says/) that almost no one uses AI agents to book rides. When a native app already reduces booking to two taps, delegating the task to an external agent adds latency and failure modes without saving effort.

## Payments: building the trust layer

For an agent to buy anything on a user's behalf, it needs an authorized payment method, a spending limit, and an auditable record of what was approved. Payment networks are positioning themselves as the trust layer for this shift so banks, merchants, and platforms do not have to rebuild checkout rails from scratch.

### Tokenization, identity, and dispute recourse

Mastercard CEO Michael Miebach has framed **Agent Pay** and **tokenization** around two core problems that appear whenever an autonomous agent transacts: credential exposure and verifiable trust.

1. **Wrapping raw credentials:** Rather than handing raw card or account numbers to a chatbot or merchant, the underlying credential is wrapped in a scoped or one-time token. If an agent endpoint is compromised, the raw payment data is not exposed.
2. **Proving agent identity and user intent:** Every agent-initiated payment has to establish two facts — that the agent is legitimate (not a spoofed bot) and that the user or organization actually authorized the specific purchase.
3. **Preserving consumer protections:** Routing agentic transactions through established network rules keeps existing cardholder guarantees and chargeback recourse intact if an agent misfires (for example, ordering ten items instead of one). Future tokens are also expected to carry richer transaction context across real-time rails.

### Consumer fragmented baskets vs. B2B machine-to-machine flows

In consumer shopping, AI discovery often surfaces niche products across many small, independent sellers rather than one marketplace. Checking out across a dozen unfamiliar sites creates severe drop-off; standardizing the payment and checkout layer (in coordination with major commerce platforms such as Google) is meant to close that gap. While total annual volume is still small, Mastercard has pointed to steep week-over-week growth from that low base.

The larger long-term opportunity, however, is **B2B and machine-to-machine commerce** (`Agent Pay for Machines`), where software agents transact directly with other agents in corporate operations — continuously buying inputs such as compute or digital ad inventory in small-dollar, real-time increments instead of settling quarterly invoices.

| Dimension | Consumer agentic commerce | B2B and machine-to-machine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary trigger | User asks an assistant to find and buy items across multiple merchants | Procurement agents autonomously buy ongoing inputs (compute, ads) |
| Cadence and size | Ad-hoc retail purchases | High-velocity, real-time, small-dollar settlement |
| Core trust requirement | Verifying agent authorization and preserving bank dispute rights | Enforcing strict spend limits, auditability, and treasury control |
| Role of tokenization | Masks raw card data during AI-assisted checkout | Moves value across cards, tokenized deposits, and push rails |

[Visa is similarly building tools for agentic commerce](https://corporate.visa.com/en/solutions/acceptance/agentic-commerce.html), though credential structures and network rules vary by implementation. It would be premature to assume every agent will run on a platform-issued commercial card or that a single interchange spread will define the market. The central economic question is who captures the take rate across the agent, merchant, issuer, and network: merchants will weigh new fees against incremental sales and fraud losses, while users need clear confirmation and instant revocation of authority.

## Travel: where technical capability hits business policy

Travel is substantially harder than food delivery or ridesharing. A single flight booking involves fare rules, baggage allowances, connection buffers, change penalties, and verified passenger identity. That complexity has triggered two competing moves: vertical consolidation by platforms, and access restrictions by suppliers.

On the consolidation side, AI travel agents have moved quickly from experiments to strategic assets:

- **OpenAI's acquisition of Odessia:** Founded less than a year earlier by Sonder founder Francis Davidson, Odessia was acquired by OpenAI to anchor its travel push. According to [industry reporting](https://karryon.com.au/industry-news/other-technology/openai-ai-travel-agent-odessia-australia/), the service had already gained meaningful early traction:

> "Over [US] \$60M/year of travel was planned by Odessia over the past summer by tens of thousands of early adopters, using AI to plan and book extraordinary trips is now a reality."

Source: [Karryon, reporting on OpenAI's acquisition of Odessia](https://karryon.com.au/industry-news/other-technology/openai-ai-travel-agent-odessia-australia/)

- **Booking Holdings' Vayo:** Booking launched its own AI travel agent, Vayo, in October 2026 with a planned USD 100 million investment. Since mid-November, Vayo has been able to book flights, hotels, and activities in a single conversation via an invite-only beta where travelers connect their email inbox — a sign that incumbent online travel agencies are racing to defend their [intermediation role](https://www.conduit.ai/blog/will-ai-agents-replace-booking-com).

On the supplier side, airlines are drawing boundaries. In November 2026, [United restricted personal AI agents](https://www.fastcompany.com/91640548/united-airlines-just-made-a-big-change-to-flight-bookings-heres-why-delta-and-american-arent-following) from booking or modifying tickets through its systems, while Delta stated it was still evaluating external agents. Access is therefore a commercial and policy decision as much as a technical one: an agent that can compare itineraries but cannot ticket or service a reservation has only solved half the problem.

## The access bottleneck: rate limits and walled gardens

Travel is not an isolated case. Large platforms and marketplaces — from [Amazon](https://www.danhock.co/p/how-scared-should-amazon-be-of-agents) to productivity suites — have strong incentives to control how external agents touch their inventory, ad surfaces, and user data, a dynamic [Paul Graham has highlighted on X](https://x.com/paulg/status/2108011311229747525).

Even when platforms expose official agent interfaces, they enforce strict quotas to prevent runaway scraping and API abuse. Google's Workspace Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers are a concrete example:

> "Across GA Google Workspace MCP servers (Gmail, Calendar, Drive, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Chat, and Tasks), the Standard Tier is capped at ==5,000 requests per day==, per project, per product (in addition to per-minute rate limits). Unlike single API calls, some MCP tools count as multiple requests because they make multiple API calls."

Source: [Google Workspace Developer Documentation, Quota and Usage Tiers](https://developers.google.com/workspace/guides/quota-usage-tiers)

For developers building autonomous workflows, a 5,000-request daily ceiling — where a single high-level tool call can consume multiple quota units — turns API governance and caching into first-order engineering constraints.

## Compute: the infrastructure angle

My long-term interest in Arm (`ARM`) is an indirect way to follow growth in the compute layer underneath these systems. Arm licenses processor architectures and earns royalties on chips incorporating its technology, reporting fiscal 2026 revenue of USD 4.92 billion in its [FY2026 Annual Report](https://investors.arm.com/static-files/29742a1f-33e5-4320-9a45-4079efcdb321) (see also its [September 2026 SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1973239/000197323926000142/arm-20260930.htm)).

<Callout title="My View" tone="thesis">
  Agents may increase demand for the CPUs that orchestrate software, tool calls, and cloud infrastructure alongside the accelerators running models, but higher agent activity does not translate mechanically into Arm revenue.

  - **Bull case:** Expanding cloud orchestration workloads and broader adoption of Arm-based server and edge silicon lift high-margin royalty revenue.
  - **Bear case:** Architectural competition, customer concentration, elevated R&D spending on new products, and valuation multiples absorb much of the upside.
</Callout>
