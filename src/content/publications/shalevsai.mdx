AI will create economic value—that much is clear. What is far less certain is whether the companies financing and building the underlying infrastructure will earn the returns they expect.

The U.S. shale gas revolution offers a useful precedent: a transformative supply expansion can lower costs and generate massive surplus for downstream users while the producers funding the build-out struggle to capture the value they create. Yet AI compute is not natural gas, and its demand curve may behave very differently. The central investment question is whether paying demand for AI services grows fast enough, and at high enough margins, to justify the capital committed to chips, data centers, power, and models.

<Callout title="The thesis" tone="thesis">
  Expanding infrastructure capacity can drive down unit prices and shift economic surplus from producers to downstream users—just as U.S. shale gas did for utilities and chemical makers. For AI infrastructure owners to avoid a similar return compression, new capabilities and lower inference costs must unlock enough paying demand to outpace capacity growth. Meanwhile, select industrial and grid-equipment suppliers can monetize the build-out while capital is being deployed.
</Callout>

## Part 1: Can we compare shale to tokens?

Throughout the 2000s, U.S. shale gas production scaled rapidly. Between 2000 and 2010, shale grew from a minor contributor to roughly 23% of domestic natural gas supply, fundamentally reshaping U.S. energy markets over the following decade.

The sequence was straightforward:

- Large capital investments in shale drilling made natural gas abundant and cheap.
- Downstream consumers and industrial users captured most of the cost savings.
- Upstream producers were not guaranteed an attractive return on that capital.

Companies poured capital into leasing acreage, drilling wells, and building pipelines and midstream infrastructure. As supply surged, domestic gas prices collapsed relative to prior peaks.

![U.S. natural gas Producer Price Index and market levels, 2007–2015 (BLS)](/publications/multi_level_2007-01-01_2015-12-31.svg)

Between 2006 and 2014, U.S. marketed natural gas production rose by roughly 35% (with dry shale gas surging more than sevenfold), driving the Henry Hub spot price down from peaks above \$8–\$13/MMBtu in 2005–2008 to around \$2–\$4/MMBtu throughout the 2010s.[^bls]

Cheaper gas spread economic benefits well beyond the exploration and production (E&P) companies:

- Power generators used lower-cost gas to produce electricity.
- Chemical and fertilizer producers used it as an advantaged feedstock.

Lower natural gas prices reduced fuel costs for power generators, industrial users, and households, although the timing and magnitude of those savings varied by region and depended on how much of the cost decline passed through to end users.

For the producers themselves, the financial toll was severe. Between 2010 and 2020, the U.S. shale sector burned roughly \$300 billion in cumulative negative free cash flow (per Deloitte and McKinsey energy analyses), as drillers continually outspent operating cash flow to lease acreage and drill wells. According to Haynes and Boone's *Oil Patch Bankruptcy Monitor*, more than 250 North American oil and gas producers filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy between 2015 and 2020, restructuring over \$170 billion in debt.[^hb]

### The Chemical and Fertilizer Renaissance

Downstream industrial users were among the clearest winners. Companies such as CF Industries (nitrogen fertilizer) and Dow Inc. (ethylene and plastics) gained a structural global cost advantage because U.S. natural gas—both their primary feedstock and energy input—traded at a steep discount to European and Asian benchmarks.

### Regulated Utilities

Electric utilities similarly capitalized on cheap domestic supply, retiring aging coal plants and shifting baseload and peaking generation toward lower-cost natural gas.

<Callout title="Key takeaway" tone="note">
  The economic value created by a new source of supply can be far greater than the profits captured by the companies producing it.
</Callout>

That does not mean every producer lost money or every gas user came out ahead. Balance-sheet discipline, hedging positions, acreage quality, and the timing of upfront capex ultimately separated winners from losers.

### How the shale revolution changed natural gas markets

> - It shifted the geography of production, increased well productivity dramatically, and introduced a measurable change in supply responsiveness after 2009.
> - It reversed long-standing expectations of U.S. import dependence and enabled the build-out of LNG export infrastructure beginning in 2016.
> - It required substantial pipeline and midstream investment as new production centers outpaced existing transport capacity.
> - And it introduced a durable linkage between domestic production, pricing and flows and the global gas market and its conditions.

Source: [RBAC, *A Short History of the Shale Revolution*](https://rbac.com/a-short-history-of-the-shale-revolution/)

### Addressing the elephant in the room

![An elephant standing in an office among knocked-over chairs, captioned "Address me"](/publications/adressme.jpeg)

The AI infrastructure build-out risks following a similar pattern:

1. Capital pours into data centers, chips, GPUs, power generation, and networking.
2. AI compute and model serving capacity expand rapidly.
3. Inference becomes dramatically cheaper.
4. Consumers and downstream enterprises benefit from cheaper intelligence and automation.
5. Infrastructure owners and model providers may earn weaker returns than their capex budgets imply.

Cheaper compute means more affordable access to GPUs, TPUs, high-bandwidth memory, and the AI services built on top of them. Downstream businesses can treat intelligence as an input, much the way a chemical manufacturer treats natural gas as a feedstock.

### Inference and Compute Costs

The price of accessing a fixed level of AI capability has fallen sharply as models, quantization, serving software, and accelerator hardware have become more efficient. At the same time, the cost of training the most capable frontier models continues to climb as labs commit larger compute clusters to push performance further. That split—falling unit inference prices alongside rising frontier capex—is central to the return-on-capital debate.

If model capabilities converge and switching costs stay low, the surplus created by AI could diffuse broadly across consumers and adopting industries rather than concentrating in a handful of infrastructure owners or frontier labs.

### Why might AI infrastructure builders not see a good ROI?

Hyperscalers and specialized cloud providers are building data centers at unprecedented scale because they expect sustained demand. Once that capacity comes online, those same players will compete for enterprise and consumer workloads with increasingly capable models.

If usable capacity expands faster than paying demand, providers may compete more aggressively on price. That dynamic would pressure utilization and gross margins—particularly for inference workloads that customers treat as interchangeable. Under that scenario, top-line AI revenue can still grow while returns on the underlying capital base disappoint.

Importantly, the economics across the stack are not identical: a chip designer, a data-center landlord, a cloud operator, and a frontier model lab face very different competitive moats and capital intensities. A decline in model API prices does not automatically compress margins for bottleneck hardware suppliers, even if it squeezes undifferentiated compute landlords.

### Unit economics of a data center

To evaluate whether a data-center investment creates value, investors have to compare the present value of cash flows over the asset's useful life against:

- Initial construction, power-interconnect, and accelerator hardware costs.
- Ongoing chip replacement, refresh, and upgrade cycles.
- Financing and power costs.
- The risk-adjusted return (WACC) required by capital providers.

Small shifts in utilization and realized pricing compound quickly at the asset level:

| Hypothetical data center | Strong utilization | Weak utilization |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Annual available capacity | 1 million GPU-hours | 1 million GPU-hours |
| Utilization | 80% | 50% |
| Revenue per sold GPU-hour | \$4.00 | \$3.00 |
| **Annual revenue** | **\$3.2 million** | **\$1.5 million** |

A 30-percentage-point drop in utilization combined with a 25% decline in realized hourly pricing cuts annual revenue by more than half—before accounting for fixed depreciation, power commitments, and debt service.

## Part 3: The Counterargument

There are two critical places where the shale analogy breaks down:

1. Natural gas and oil are not tokens.
2. Falling supply costs were not the sole driver of the 2014 oil price collapse—weakening demand expectations, fewer supply disruptions, and OPEC's decision to maintain output all played a role alongside rising U.S. production.

Shale gas entered a mature market for heating, electricity, and industrial feedstocks. **AI compute is entering a much broader and less mature market.**

As models improve and unit costs fall, tasks that were previously uneconomic—or impossible to automate at scale—become viable. Total demand for compute could therefore expand faster than the cost of serving each individual task falls.

### Demand Growth vs. Price Elasticity

The deeper difference lies in how demand responds to capability and price. Two distinct economic mechanisms are easy to conflate:

- **Demand growth** means people and enterprises want more AI over time because its capabilities, reliability, and product integrations are expanding (an outward shift of the demand curve).
- **Price elasticity** measures how much more AI people use strictly because its price falls, holding capabilities and other factors constant (movement along the demand curve).

![Demand growth for AI: outward demand shift raising equilibrium price and quantity](/publications/AIDemandGrowthCurve.svg)

AI benefits from both forces simultaneously: rapid underlying demand growth and high sensitivity to falling unit prices. Rising token volume alone does not isolate pure price elasticity, because models are also getting smarter and being embedded into more workflows at the same time.

Conventional oil demand, by contrast, grows gradually with GDP, population, travel, and industrial activity while remaining relatively price-inelastic in the short run—drivers cannot immediately replace their vehicles or redesign supply chains when fuel prices move. That low short-run responsiveness in both supply and demand is why energy markets experience sharp price swings.

![Price elasticity of AI demand: lower prices increasing quantity demanded along the curve](/publications/PriceElasticityofAIDemand.svg)

With AI, falling prices and rising capability can unlock entirely new categories of work:

- A company that could afford to audit only a sample of customer interactions can analyze all of them.
- A research team can run thousands of hypotheses or simulations rather than a handful.
- A software organization can shift from occasional coding assistance to agents that continuously test, refactor, and maintain codebases.
- A financial institution can parse every filing, contract, and earnings call rather than only its highest-priority coverage.

That is a fundamentally broader demand response than driving slightly more because gasoline is cheaper.

### Frontier FOMO

Even when near-term AI monetization is uncertain, strategic competition can compel companies and governments to keep investing in compute because the cost of falling behind may be existential.

As models improve and compute scales, researchers and enterprises can tackle problems that were previously out of reach. That sets off a self-reinforcing investment loop:

1. Models improve.
2. More complex tasks become feasible.
3. Enterprises recognize the strategic value of those capabilities.
4. Demand for compute rises.
5. Infrastructure investment accelerates to meet that demand.
6. Hardware and software stack efficiency improves to serve frontier models at lower cost.
7. Lower unit costs and better tooling unlock the next wave of applications, restarting the cycle.

### Cheaper AI can lead to more overall compute demand

Consider a simple illustrative comparison in which algorithmic and hardware efficiency improves tenfold in both scenarios:

| | Today | Base case | Bull case |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Useful AI tasks performed | 1 billion | 5 billion | 20 billion |
| Compute required per task | 1 unit | 0.1 units | 0.1 units |
| **Total compute required** | **1 billion units** | **0.5 billion units** | **2 billion units** |

- In the **base case**, task volume grows fivefold, so total compute demand falls by half.
- In the **bull case**, task volume grows twentyfold, so total compute demand doubles despite the tenfold efficiency gain.

These figures are illustrative, not forecasts. They also hold task complexity constant. In practice, task mix shifts continuously: longer context windows, test-time reasoning, video generation, and multi-step agent workflows consume far more compute per task than simple text queries, even as hardware and software efficiencies push unit costs down. Total compute demand depends on the net balance of both forces. See Microsoft Research on [energy use of AI inference, efficiency pathways, and test-time scaling](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/energy-use-of-ai-inference-efficiency-pathways-and-test-time-scaling/).

## Part 4: Capturing Value

### Beneficiaries of building AI infrastructure

Even if long-run returns on hyperscale compute remain debated, physical-layer suppliers can earn attractive margins while capital is being deployed. Key categories include:

- Construction materials and structural steel.
- Heavy equipment rentals.
- Power systems, grid-edge intelligence, and electrical equipment.
- Liquid cooling, thermal management, water treatment, and building controls.
- Engineering and specialized infrastructure services.

The core investment question for these picks-and-shovels suppliers is:

> Can these companies earn attractive returns while data centers are being built, and how exposed are they if hyperscale spending slows?

#### Nucor Corporation (`NUE`)

Nucor supplies structural and fabricated steel products used in data-center construction and explicitly markets its solutions to data-center builders. The key diligence questions are how material data-center tonnage is relative to total company shipments, how steel cyclicality and scrap spreads affect margins through the cycle, and whether current valuation already prices in peak non-residential construction demand.

![Nucor (NUE) one-year total return](/publications/NUE_total_return_1y.svg)

#### ITT Inc. (`ITT`)

As AI server racks transition from air cooling to direct-to-chip liquid cooling and rear-door heat exchangers—driven by high-density GPU thermal loads—data centers require large-scale chilled-water circulation, coolant distribution units (CDUs), and backup generator fuel-transfer pumps. ITT's Industrial Process (IP) segment, including brands such as Goulds Pumps and MP Pumps, supplies centrifugal and specialty pumps used in data-center thermal management and power generation.

For investors, the key variables to track are the share of IP backlog tied to data-center HVAC and liquid cooling versus traditional oil, gas, chemical, and mining end-markets, and whether operating margin expansion justifies the stock's forward multiple.

#### Acuity Inc. (`AYI`)

Acuity offers potential exposure through intelligent building controls and data-center environmental monitoring. In October 2026, Acuity announced an agreement to acquire Parameter Technologies, a data-center monitoring and leak-detection business (pending closing at announcement). Evaluating the opportunity requires weighing direct data-center revenue exposure against acquisition economics, competitive intensity in building management systems, and integration execution.

![Acuity (AYI) one-year total return](/publications/AYI_total_return_1y.svg)

#### Herc Holdings Inc. (`HRI`)

Equipment rental companies benefit directly from multi-year, site-intensive construction projects, and Herc has highlighted data centers as a meaningful growth driver in its large-project pipeline. Key diligence factors include the share of revenue tied to data-center builds, fleet utilization and time-on-rent trends, capital expenditure discipline, balance-sheet leverage, and downside sensitivity if broader non-residential construction slows.

![Herc Holdings (HRI) one-year total return](/publications/HRI_total_return_1y.svg)

#### Itron, Inc. (`ITRI`)

AI data centers are creating severe localized bottlenecks on the electric grid. Because utilities cannot permit and build high-voltage transmission lines fast enough to match hyperscale timelines, they are turning to grid-edge intelligence, smart metering, and distributed load-management software and hardware—the core of Itron's Outcomes and Networked Solutions segments. The primary risks to monitor are utility capex approval timelines (public utility commissions move far more slowly than private hyperscalers), backlog conversion velocity, and the mix shift between hardware and higher-margin recurring software.

![Itron (ITRI) one-year total return](/publications/ITRI_total_return_1y.svg)

#### Veralto Corporation (`VLTO`)

High-density data centers also depend on reliable industrial water treatment, purification, and cooling-loop chemistry to prevent scaling, corrosion, and thermal inefficiency. Veralto's water quality and treatment businesses sit directly in that operational workflow, offering recurring consumables and instrumentation exposure tied to both new facility commissioning and ongoing uptime—though investors must assess how large data-center demand is relative to its broader municipal and industrial water base.

![Veralto (VLTO) one-year total return](/publications/VLTO_total_return_1y.svg)

## Part 5: Concluding Thoughts

1. **Fast demand growth does not prevent overbuilding.** Even in a booming market, capital can flood in faster than paying demand materializes.
2. **Supply and demand can both grow rapidly while supply still outpaces demand.** If paying demand for AI compute grows 50% while usable capacity doubles, utilization and pricing will still come under pressure.
3. **What matters is the balance between the two.** Neither supply growth nor demand growth in isolation determines returns; the spread between them sets asset utilization and margins.
4. **If demand outruns capacity, returns can stay strong.** If new capabilities and falling prices unlock enough new workloads to outpace supply additions, pricing and utilization can remain resilient—leaving timing and eventual market size as the central uncertainties.
