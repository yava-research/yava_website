## Macro backdrop: growth versus execution

In its April 2026 update, the World Bank estimated India's real GDP growth at **7.6%** in fiscal year 2025–26 and projected **6.6%** for 2026–27.[^wb-update] That forecast is a snapshot, not a promise: energy prices, external demand, and the pace of capital formation can shift the outcome. Sustaining momentum also requires creating more productive jobs as the workforce expands.

The structural opportunity is broad. More reliable power, logistics, and credit lift economy-wide productivity; formal businesses can capture share from fragmented informal markets; and domestic manufacturers can take a larger role in industrial supply chains. The constraint is execution. A large addressable market matters little to minority shareholders if intense competition erodes margins, borrowing costs rise, or companies overpay for capacity.

## Three questions that will decide equity returns

Looking past headline GDP, three questions will determine how broadly India's expansion translates into shareholder value:

1. **Private capex follow-through:** Can private corporate investment step in behind public infrastructure spending?
2. **Job creation and productivity:** Can manufacturing and services generate enough productive employment to sustain income growth?[^wb-india]
3. **Credit-disciplined consumption:** Can household income growth support discretionary spending without relying excessively on unsecured retail credit?

The answers will vary sharply by sector and region. A strong macro backdrop can still coexist with disappointing equity returns—especially over shorter holding periods—when starting valuations are full, the rupee weakens, or governance and equity dilution dilute per-share compounding. The basket below is a structured way to test this thesis, not a claim that every name will benefit equally.

## Where growth is concentrating

### Defense and industrial capacity

India's push to localize defense manufacturing offers a multi-year runway for shipbuilders, defense electronics suppliers, and precision engineers. The government reported defense exports of **₹23,622 crore** in fiscal 2024–25.[^mod] Order books are encouraging, but investors must track how cleanly backlog converts into cash, delivery schedules, and concentration risk around government procurement.

Industrial demand extends well beyond defense. Power generation, cooling, compressors, and basic materials are essential as factories and urban grids expand. Even so, these businesses remain cyclical: industry-wide capacity additions, raw-material swings, and capital intensity can overwhelm a favorable secular trend.

### Consumer and financial services

Rising household incomes and digital distribution continue to favor branded consumer goods, beauty, quick commerce, healthcare, and financial services. The central question is unit economics—whether customer acquisition and fulfillment costs leave adequate operating margin once competition intensifies. Likewise, lenders and insurers must maintain underwriting and credit discipline; loan or policy growth alone is not evidence of value creation.

### Scoring methodology

The original proposal targeted November 1, 2026 for initiation. Because that date fell on a Sunday, the reproducible *hypothetical* baseline uses the **November 2, 2026** closing price on each specified exchange, subject to confirming each security traded that day. The scheduled review date is **March 31, 2027** (calendar Q1 end). No starting prices are assumed to be verified here.

The basket's rupee total return will be measured as ending market value plus cash distributions, divided by starting value, minus one—adjusting for splits, bonus issues, mergers, and other corporate actions, and documenting the price source alongside any suspended or delisted names. Performance will be benchmarked against the **Nifty 50 Total Return Index** over the identical window, before fees and taxes. Note that equal share counts (50 shares per name) are **not equal weights**: higher-priced stocks carry larger initial capital weights. Until baseline and review-date prices are collected, no return can be reported.

## Risks and what could go wrong

- **Valuation and multiple compression:** Paying peak multiples for domestic growth themes leaves little margin of safety if earnings growth normalizes or global risk appetite turns.
- **Currency and macro shocks:** Energy import bills, global trade friction, or rupee depreciation can erode USD-denominated returns even when local-currency earnings hold up.
- **Cycle and execution risk:** Defense and industrial names face lumpy government awards and working-capital swings, while metals, refining, and shipping remain tied to global commodity cycles.
- **Methodology caveats:** Because the basket uses equal share counts rather than equal rupee weights, a handful of high-nominal-price stocks can dominate total basket return.
